In 2020, a research ship surveyed the floor of the Gulf of Aqaba with multibeam sonar. At one thousand seven hundred and seventy meters, the survey showed no sign of what was there.

There was a lake down there. Two hundred and sixty meters long, with a surface, and a shoreline that the brine washes over whenever something disturbs it.

It was found on a robot dive. And it took the robot's own imaging sonar to pick out its edges, because the lake bounced the sound back strangely soft.

What the robot's lights showed next is why this place matters. Along the shore, shrimp were working. Some went more than thirty meters out over the lake to collect animals the water had stunned or killed. An eel kept dipping into the brine and pulling back out.

The water in that lake is about four times saltier than the sea above it, and fifty centimeters below its surface there is almost no oxygen at all. Nothing digs in its mud. Nothing stirs it.

And that is the part the scientists cared about most. Because mud that nothing touches keeps a record. In this case, a record of floods, earthquakes and, most likely, tsunamis, going back at least twelve hundred years. The youngest of its coarse layers may line up with a magnitude seven point two earthquake that shook this coast in 1995.

So how do you miss a lake? And what else is written in the mud at the bottom of it?

=====

One note before we go down. The team that found this lake filmed it, but that footage isn't free to use. The underwater shots in this video come from NOAA Ocean Exploration, mostly from brine pools in the Gulf of Mexico. They show the same kind of place, on the other side of the world. Almost every number about this lake comes from one paper: Purkis and colleagues, published in 2022 in Communications Earth and Environment. Where we use other sources, we say so. The links are in the description.

=====

The Gulf of Aqaba is the narrow northeastern arm of the Red Sea. Egypt's Sinai Peninsula is on one side. Saudi Arabia is on the other.

Near the Saudi coast, the floor of the gulf drops into a basin called the Aragonese Deep. It is more than eight hundred meters deeper than the gulf's average. Two large faults run along its sides, and the crust between them is being pulled apart. Geologists call this a pull-apart basin. It is the only one in the gulf.

That geometry has a cost. Earthquakes above magnitude five strike this area. In 1995, one of these faults ruptured in a major earthquake. Keep that year in mind.

The lake sits at the very foot of the Saudi slope, two kilometers from the coast. Its eastern shore is the slope itself, rising at thirty-five degrees. Follow that slope up through one point seven kilometers of water and you reach a coral reef in full sunlight.

Lakes like this are rare. Brine pools are known from only three seas: the Gulf of Mexico, the Mediterranean, and the Red Sea. Across all three, scientists have found a few dozen. This one was the first found outside the Red Sea proper. And no other brine pool in the region sits so close to land. The next closest is twenty-five kilometers offshore.

That closeness to shore turns out to matter more than anything else about it. We'll get there.

=====

First, the miss.

The ship was OceanXplorer, an eighty-seven-meter research vessel. Its hull carried multibeam sonar, which sweeps a fan of sound across the seafloor and builds a depth map from the echoes. The system was set up to resolve features about ten meters across.

The paper states the result in one dry sentence. The pools "lacked a multibeam signature."

It doesn't say why. One guess, ours and not the paper's: the lake is only six meters deep at its center, and its surface is flat. A flat layer of liquid filling a shallow dip may look, to a ship one point seven kilometers above, like ordinary flat seafloor.

So the lake was found the slow way. A robot went down and looked. According to the University of Miami, the team's leader, Sam Purkis, said the discovery came in the last five minutes of the ten-hour dive they could dedicate to this project.

Once they knew where to look, the robot's own sonar could see it. That sonar works at much higher frequencies, four hundred and fifty to nine hundred kilohertz. The lake showed up as a patch of "dampened backscatter." In plain terms, the brine sent back a weaker echo than the mud around it. That weak echo is how they mapped its edges and steered around it.

Then they did something that sounds impossible. They parked the robot on the lake.

=====

The brine is so dense that the team landed their remotely operated vehicle on its surface. The paper gives the robot's weight as ten thousand pounds, about four and a half tonnes on deck. The brine was dense enough for it to settle on.

From that perch, they lowered instruments straight through the boundary. Dye released at the surface showed how sharp it is. Seawater above, brine below, and a visible line between them.

The numbers across that line are stark.

Above it, the water was a steady twenty-one point three degrees, with normal Red Sea salinity and normal oxygen, all the way down to the surface of the lake, at one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine point four six meters.

Fifteen centimeters below the surface, the salinity had already pushed past the top of the probe's range. Lab tests later put it at about four times that of seawater.

Oxygen fell by more than three-quarters right at the boundary. Fifty centimeters down, it was below what the probe could reliably measure.

Temperature barely moved. Two meters into the brine, the water was only one degree warmer than the sea above. That small rise suggests to the team that no hydrothermal heat source sits nearby.

Think of a glass with syrup at the bottom and water poured gently on top. The two sit in layers. Here, the syrup is a brine of dissolved salt, and the glass is a dip in the floor of the gulf.

Which raises the obvious question. Where does that much salt come from, two kilometers from a desert coast?

=====

At least part of the answer was leaking out of the slope.

Along the lake's eastern shore, the robot found brine seeping from the base of the slope, at points up to five meters above the lake surface. The seeps appear every two to three meters along the full two hundred and sixty meters where the lake meets the slope. Each one sends a thin curtain of brine sliding downhill into the pool. When the robot's arm disturbed the stained mud, brine came shimmering out of it.

The team then asked what kind of salt this is. There are two candidates. The first is seawater that evaporated on the coast and soaked down through the rock. The second is ancient salt, buried under the seafloor and slowly dissolving.

Evaporation leaves a fingerprint. As water evaporates, heavier oxygen stays behind. Seawater concentrated to this brine's saltiness should carry a strong heavy-oxygen signal. This brine barely has one.

So the authors favor the second option. Beneath the gulf lie thick layers of salt laid down more than five million years ago, when the young Red Sea basin partly dried out. Water dissolving that buried salt, and seeping out at the foot of the slope, is the most plausible source.

The brine has one more chemical oddity. It holds less sulfate, relative to its chloride, than any other brine measured in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aqaba system. Something in the lake has been consuming it. That something is alive.

=====

Over six weeks of dives in this gulf, the team describes the deep floor as a desolate seabed, thickly draped with mud. Few species. Little variety.

The lake is the exception.

Its shore is laid out in rings. Furthest out is ordinary mud. Then a gray band, one to two meters wide, stained by a dense film of microbes. Then an orange band right at the waterline, where the brine washes back and forth. Inside the lake, the microbes change. The ones that need oxygen give way to microbes that live without it, including bacteria that feed on sulfate. Those are the ones eating the sulfate out of the brine.

In a twenty-centimeter band just above the surface lives a black clam, Apachecorbula muriatica. It has been found living this way, right at the edge of the brine, around another Red Sea brine pool. The team suggests that this clam, and the animals around it, may ultimately depend on the microbes, which make their energy from chemistry instead of sunlight.

Then there are the hunters.

Eels, a shark called the bigeye houndshark, and flatfish were seen cruising the surface of the brine. The eel kept dipping into it, the authors write, presumably to retrieve animals that had been "shocked or killed" by the water.

The shrimp left less room for doubt. They gathered in large numbers on high points around the rim. Filmed under red light, which lets researchers watch deep-sea animals without disturbing them, the shrimp ventured as far as the middle of the lake, more than thirty meters from shore. There, they plucked out small animals the brine had disabled.

Small creatures that end up in the brine are stunned or killed, and the hunters along the edge pick them off.

Microbes from other Red Sea brine pools have already yielded molecules with antibacterial and anticancer potential. That alone makes these places worth studying.

But for this team, the biggest surprise was in the mud.

=====

They pushed tubes into the lakebed along a line from the ordinary seabed into the brine.

The core taken outside the lake was a mess. Burrowing animals had churned it. The cores from under the brine looked completely different. No oxygen means no burrowers. Layers only a millimeter thick were still intact.

The longest core reached more than a meter into the lakebed. Radiocarbon dates from six levels put its base at around twelve hundred years old. The dates were in order from top to bottom, with no reversals. That suggests a continuous record. The authors add one caveat: old carbon recycled into the mud could skew some dates.

Now, why would a brine pool keep such a clean record? The answer is the same density that let them land the robot.

When a muddy flow pours down the slope, the authors argue, it should stall at the surface of the brine, as if it had hit a floor. Then the grains settle through the brine one size at a time. The heaviest grains reach the bottom first. The finest dust settles last. And because the brine absorbs the force of the flow, nothing below gets scoured away.

Picture a handful of sand and dust dropped into a tall jar of syrup. The sand lands first, the dust drifts down on top, and the layers already on the bottom stay exactly where they were. The brine also shields the lakebed from deep currents that would otherwise sweep it.

So what's in the layers?

=====

The core shows two kinds of event layer, plus signs of shaking.

The first is coarse. Rose-colored layers of sand and small gravel, made of quartz and feldspar. These grains come from land. The slope above the lake is built from reef debris and lime mud, so they did not simply slide down from next door. Something carried them off the coast and down one point seven kilometers.

There are ten of these coarse layers in the last thousand years. That's roughly one per century. The thickest is thirty centimeters, dated to roughly six centuries ago.

The second kind is finer. At least fifty thin layers, each grading from sand at the bottom to mud at the top. They're the signature of underwater sediment flows. The authors compare them to deposits from flash floods, when rare desert storms send muddy water rushing out of dry valleys into the gulf. Fifty in twelve hundred years is at least four per century.

And in three places, the layers are bent and contorted. The authors say that is consistent with earthquake shaking.

=====

The coarse layers are the puzzle. How does gravel from land end up at the bottom of a deep basin?

The authors list five ways. Rivers: there are no permanent rivers flowing into this hyper-arid gulf. Wind: the grains are too coarse to blow there. Storm waves: the gulf is too narrow to build waves large enough. Flash floods: possible, but the authors argue the grains are coarser than flood deposits would leave at this depth.

That leaves tsunamis. The authors call them, quote, "to our minds the most plausible" explanation.

Two dates support the idea. The authors consider the radiocarbon age of the youngest coarse layer consistent with the 1995 Nuweiba earthquake, given how large the dating error is for such young material. That earthquake struck on the twenty-second of November 1995, with a magnitude of seven point two according to the US Geological Survey. According to the paper, it ruptured part of a fault inside this same basin and generated a modest tsunami.

The second date is older. The thickest layer, from roughly six centuries ago, broadly matches an underwater landslide in the Straits of Tiran, eighty kilometers south. Earlier work by the same lead author suggests that landslide may have sent a tsunami up the gulf toward this coast.

Neither match proves a tsunami. They are dates that line up, which is how a case like this starts.

=====

Here is what the paper leaves open.

First, the tsunamis themselves. "Most plausible" is not proven. The coarse layers could still have another cause.

Second, the floods and the earthquakes. The fine layers could come from flash floods, from slope failures shaken loose by earthquakes, or from both. Telling them apart, the authors write, lies beyond the scope of this paper. The plan is to compare the dated layers with the historical earthquake record of the gulf, which goes back to 1517.

Third, the oxygen. The probe's readings at the bottom were below what it could reliably measure. The brine may be completely without oxygen, or it may get a trickle from the seawater above. The data can't yet tell those apart.

Fourth, the surface of the lake itself. The salinity jump at the boundary appears sharper than you'd expect if brine and seawater had been sitting together quietly for a long time. That could mean brine arrives in sudden pulses. One possible trigger is movement on the fault the lake sits on. The authors say their measurements aren't fine enough to be sure.

There is one way to find out: go back and measure the same lake again, over years. Until someone does, the most recent pages of this record are still unread.
